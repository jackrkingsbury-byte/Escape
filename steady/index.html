Anger & aggression

Cool the fire

Anger is a normal emotion. It tells you a line has been crossed or something you need is blocked. Aggression is what you do with it: shouting, threatening, breaking things, hitting, cutting someone down. You can’t stop the feeling arriving. You can widen the gap between feeling it and acting on it.

Catch it early

Anger builds in steps. In the build-up you can still think, so that’s where every technique works. Learn your early warning signs so you notice at 4 out of 10, not 9.

Venting doesn’t drain it

People who hit a punch bag while thinking about the person who angered them were more aggressive afterwards than people who sat quietly (Bushman, 2002). Calming the body and changing the story work better.

The story is the fuel

“They did it on purpose” and “they always do this” keep anger burning long after the moment. Checking the story is a core skill in anger-management therapy, which helps most people who try it (Beck & Fernandez, 1998).

Cooling down takes time

After a flare-up your body stays primed and small things set it off again. When you’re flooded, take at least 20 minutes before you try to talk, as couples researcher John Gottman recommends.

Say it straight

Assertive means saying what you need clearly and respectfully. It gets you heard more often than either swallowing it or attacking.

Passive

“It’s fine. Whatever.”

The need goes unsaid and the resentment builds.

Aggressive

“You never listen. You’re so selfish.”

An attack on the person. They defend instead of hearing you.

Assertive

“When you look at your phone while I’m talking, I feel brushed off. Could we put phones away for ten minutes?”

Names the behaviour, the feeling and a fair request.

Say it in a low, slow voice, once you’re below 4 out of 10.

The long game

Sleep

Short sleep makes everyone more irritable and quicker to read threat into neutral faces. Protect 7 to 9 hours.

Watch the alcohol

Alcohol weakens the brakes you need most when you’re angry. If arguments tend to happen after drinking, that’s the pattern to break first.

Move every day

Exercise burns off stress arousal and lowers your baseline tension, so it takes more to tip you over.

Check HALT

Before a hard conversation, ask: am I hungry, angry, lonely or tired? If yes, deal with that first.

Fix repeat triggers

If the same thing sets you off every week, solve it at a calm time instead of gritting your teeth through it again.

Get trained

Anger-management courses based on CBT work for most people. If your anger has frightened or hurt someone, see Get help.

Anxiety & worry

Quiet the alarm

Anxiety is your body’s threat alarm going off when there’s no fire. A racing heart, tight chest and churning stomach are built to protect you. They feel awful but they aren’t dangerous, and they always pass. The aim isn’t to never feel anxious. It’s to stop letting the alarm make your decisions.

It’s a false alarm, not a fault

A panic attack peaks within minutes and then passes, even if you do nothing. Saying “this is anxiety, and it will pass” takes away some of its power.

Avoidance feeds it

Every escape brings relief, and the relief teaches you to escape again. Approaching what you fear in small steps is the most reliable way to shrink it.

Worry feels useful, rarely is

Solve what you can act on. Park the rest for a short daily worry slot. Postponing worry this way reduces how much time you spend worrying (McGowan & Behar, 2013).

Breathe out longer

A long exhale slows the heart. Five minutes a day of “cyclic sighing” lifted mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation in one trial (Balban et al., 2023).

          The long game

          Move your body

          Regular aerobic exercise reduces anxiety symptoms, and a racing heart from exercise teaches you that the sensation itself is safe.

          Cut the caffeine

          Caffeine produces the same jitters and racing heart as anxiety. Try a week with less and see what changes.

          Drop safety habits

          Constant checking, seeking reassurance and over-preparing bring short relief but keep the alarm sensitive. Cut them back one at a time.

          Practise while calm

          Breathing and grounding are skills. Two minutes a day when you’re fine makes them available when you’re not.

          Protect sleep

          Poor sleep and anxiety feed each other. A fixed wake-up time is the single most useful sleep habit.

          Get help if it’s running your life

          CBT is the recommended first treatment for anxiety disorders (NICE, 2011), and medication is an option to discuss with a doctor.

          Discipline & self-control

          Keep your word to yourself

          Discipline isn’t a trait you either have or don’t. It’s mostly design: a few decisions made in advance, surroundings that make the right thing easy, and a plan for getting back on track after you slip. Willpower is the backup, not the engine.

          Disciplined people resist less

          People with high self-control report fewer temptations, not more battles won (Hofmann et al., 2012). Their routines do the work, so they rarely need willpower at all (Galla & Duckworth, 2015).

          Decide once, in advance

          “When it’s 7am and the kettle’s on, I’ll do ten push-ups.” Across 94 studies, if-then plans like this had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

          Make it tiny

          Start with a version so small it’s hard to skip: one push-up, one line, one page. A habit has to exist before it can grow.

          Be kind after a slip

          People who met a failure with self-compassion were more motivated to improve than those who beat themselves up (Breines & Chen, 2012). Harsh self-talk tends to end in “I’ve blown it anyway”.

              The long game

              Never miss twice

              One missed day is noise. Two is the start of a new habit. After a miss, do the two-minute version the next day, whatever happens.

              Pair it with a treat

              Only listen to your favourite podcast at the gym. “Temptation bundling” like this raised gym visits in a field study (Milkman et al., 2014).

              Become the person

              Frame habits as identity: “I’m someone who trains” rather than “I’m trying to get fit”. Each repetition is a vote for that person.

              Guard the basics

              Sleep, food and movement are what self-control runs on. When those slip, everything feels harder. That’s not a character flaw.

              One thing at a time

              Pick one habit until it’s automatic, then add the next. Five changes at once is how New Year’s resolutions die.

              Make it visible

              A ticked grid or a streak on the fridge turns invisible progress into something you don’t want to break.

              Get help

              You don’t have to do this alone

              This site is a self-help toolkit. It isn’t therapy or a diagnosis. The tools here come from proven treatments, and a trained professional can tailor them to you.

              Get help now if…

              • you’re thinking about hurting yourself or someone else
              • you feel out of control or unsafe
              • someone is in danger right now
              • Emergency services112 (EU and much of the world) · 999 (UK) · 911 (US, Canada)
              • US: Suicide & Crisis LifelineCall or text 988
              • UK & Ireland: Samaritans, 24/7116 123
              • Anywhere elseFind a free helpline in your country at findahelpline.com

              See a professional if…

              • anger or anxiety has been getting in the way of work, school, sleep or relationships for weeks
              • your anger has frightened, threatened or hurt someone, or you’ve broken things
              • you’re having panic attacks, or avoiding more and more places and people
              • you’re using alcohol, drugs, gaming or food to cope

              A family doctor is a good first stop. Ask about CBT (for anxiety and anger), DBT skills groups (for intense emotions) and anger-management programmes. If your anger is aimed at a partner or family, specialist programmes exist to help you stop, and your doctor or a helpline can point you to one.
