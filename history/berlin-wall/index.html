Section 1
Definitions

Key words you need to use

Cut these out and place them down the side of your chart, or keep them as a glossary box. The German term is given where the German word is the one historians actually use.

Berlin Wall Berliner Mauer
The barrier built by East Germany from 13 August 1961 that sealed West Berlin off from East Berlin and the surrounding East German countryside. About 155 km long in total.
East Germany GDR / DDR
The German Democratic Republic, 1949–1990. A communist state allied to the Soviet Union and run by the SED party.
West Germany FRG / BRD
The Federal Republic of Germany, 1949–1990. A capitalist democracy allied to the USA, Britain and France.
Cold War
The hostility between the Soviet bloc and the Western allies from 1947 to 1991 — fought through spying, propaganda, arms races and proxy wars rather than direct battle.
Iron Curtain
Churchill's 1946 phrase for the closed frontier dividing communist Eastern Europe from the West. The Berlin Wall became its most visible section.
Fleeing the republic Republikflucht
The East German term for leaving the country without permission. Treated as a crime against the state, not as emigration.
Checkpoint Charlie
The crossing point on Friedrichstrasse used by Allied soldiers, diplomats and foreigners. Site of the October 1961 tank stand-off.
Death strip Todesstreifen
The cleared, floodlit ground between the two walls: raked sand, patrol road, watchtowers and guards with orders to shoot.
Order to fire Schießbefehl
The standing instruction to East German border guards to use firearms to stop people crossing. Denied by the GDR at the time; proved from its own files afterwards.
Ruling party SED
The Socialist Unity Party — the only party that really held power in East Germany. Led by Walter Ulbricht, then Erich Honecker.
Peaceful Revolution Die Wende
The mass protests across East Germany in autumn 1989 that forced the government to give way — without the army firing on the crowds.
Primary source
Evidence made at the time by someone who was there — a photograph, a newspaper of that date, a speech, an official file.
Secondary source
Something written later that studies the primary evidence — a history book, a museum database, a documentary.
Reunification
The joining of East and West Germany into one country on 3 October 1990, eleven months after the Wall opened.

Section 2 — Evidence
13 August 1961

One morning, photographed by both sides

Sources 1 and 2 show the same event on the same day — but one was taken for American intelligence and one by the East German state. Compare what each chose to show.

Source 01
East German tanks at the Warschauer Bridge
TypePhotographIntelligence file
Photo — Source 01CIA, 13 Aug 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 13 August
SOURCE CIA / US National Archives
FROM “A City Torn Apart: Building of the Berlin Wall”

Taken on the first morning of the closure. The GDR sent tanks, not only police — evidence that the government expected resistance and was ready to use military force against its own citizens. But this is an American intelligence photograph, chosen for a US publication: it was selected to show communist aggression, so it is reliable for what happened and less reliable for how it felt to Berliners.

archives.gov/files/research/foreign-policy/cold-war/1961-berlin-crisis/berlin-publication.pdf
Source 02
Workers' militia sealing the Brandenburg Gate
TypePhotographState archive
Photo — Source 02Bundesarchiv, 13 Aug 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 13 August
SOURCE German Federal Archives (Bundesarchiv)
REF Bild 183-85458-0002 · Photo: Peter Heinz Junge

An official East German photograph. It shows the armed “combat groups of the working class” with water cannon waiting behind them. Its value is that it comes from the communist side: it shows how the GDR wanted the operation to look — orderly, defensive, and carried out by workers rather than by soldiers. Set against Source 01, it proves that the same event can be photographed to mean two opposite things.

Section 2 — Evidence
Newspapers

What the newspapers printed that week

Two papers from two countries, written before anybody knew a permanent wall would follow. Notice that neither of them uses the word “wall”.

Source 03
“Berlin border closed” — The Observer
TypeNewspaperBritish press
Photo — Source 03CIA, 13 Aug 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 13 August
SOURCE The Observer, republished in the Guardian archive

“The East-West Berlin border was closed early to-day. Lorryloads of troops were seen taking up positions along the border near the Brandenburg Gate…”

Written the same day, in the present tense, by a reporter who did not yet know what he was watching. That is exactly why it is useful: it captures the event as “a border closing”, not as “the Berlin Wall”. The photograph beside it shows the West Berlin reaction the paper describes.

theguardian.com/world/from-the-archive-blog/2021/aug/12/berliners-wake-to-a-divided-city-wall-1961
Source 04
A neutral country reports — Neue Zürcher Zeitung
TypeNewspaperNeutral country
Photo — Source 04CIA, 14 Aug 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 14 August
SOURCE Neue Zürcher Zeitung (Zurich, Switzerland)
VIA German History in Documents and Images

“Between Potsdamer Platz and Brandenburg Gate the street was torn up and a stone wall was erected.”

Switzerland was neutral in the Cold War, so this paper had no government line to follow — which normally makes it more trustworthy. Yet it still calls the closure a “despotic act” and East Germany “Ulbricht's prison state”. Useful proof that neutral does not mean neither side: you should still check the language a source uses.

germanhistorydocs.org/en/two-germanies-1961-1989/a-neutrals-description-of-the-building-of-the-wall-august-14-1961

Section 2 — Evidence
1961

When sources disagree, and when they nearly fired

Source 05
UPI wire report: “W. Berlins jeer, E. Germans fire tear gas”
TypeNews agencyWire report
Photo — Source 05CIA, Aug 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 14 August
SOURCE United Press International · Joseph R. Fleming

A reporter filing within hours. He records that the crossing points were cut from 80 to 12 and that 50,000 East Berliners lost their jobs in the West. But the Neue Zürcher Zeitung (Source 04) says 60,000 commuters, and UPI's own follow-up says 87 crossings were sealed leaving 13. The numbers do not match. That is not a mistake in your research — it is the evidence itself telling you that first reports are counted in a hurry and must be checked against later records.

upi.com/Archives/1961/08/14/W-Berlins-jeer-E-Germans-fire-tear-gas/9541408882533/
Source 06
American tanks facing East Berlin, Friedrichstrasse
TypePhotographIntelligence file
Photo — Source 06CIA, 25 Oct 1961
YEAR 1961  ·  DATE 25 October
SOURCE CIA / US National Archives
PLACE Checkpoint Charlie, Friedrichstrasse

Ten weeks after the border closed, American and Soviet tanks stood gun barrel to gun barrel for sixteen hours over whether US officials could enter East Berlin. This is the strongest evidence that the Wall was not only a German problem: it was a point where the Cold War came closest to becoming a shooting war in Europe.

archives.gov/files/research/foreign-policy/cold-war/1961-berlin-crisis/berlin-publication.pdf

Section 2 — Evidence
1963 · 1986

Living with the Wall

Source 07
President Kennedy: “Ich bin ein Berliner”
TypeSpeechFilm archive
Photo — Source 07JFK Library, 26 Jun 1963
YEAR 1963  ·  DATE 26 June
SOURCE John F. Kennedy Presidential Library
REF USG-2B-1 · Rathaus Schöneberg, West Berlin

“Freedom is indivisible, and when one man is enslaved, all are not free.”

Delivered two years after the Wall went up, to a crowd of hundreds of thousands. Remember what kind of source this is: a political speech, written to reassure West Berliners and to warn Moscow. It is strong evidence of Western policy and morale — it tells you almost nothing about daily life in the East.

jfklibrary.org/learn/about-jfk/historic-speeches/remarks-at-the-rudolph-wilde-platz-berlin
Source 08
The painted Wall — Thierry Noir at Bethaniendamm
TypePhotographArt / culture
Photo — Source 08Thierry Noir, 1986
YEAR 1986
SOURCE Photograph by Thierry Noir, the artist
PLACE Bethaniendamm, Berlin-Kreuzberg

By the 1980s the western face of the Wall was covered in paint. The detail that matters for your argument: only the western side could be painted. West Berliners could walk right up and touch it; anyone approaching from the East faced the death strip and the guards. The graffiti is therefore evidence in itself of which side had freedom of movement.

commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Berlinermauer.jpg

Section 2 — Evidence
1987 · 1989

The end — demanded, then announced by mistake

Source 09
President Reagan at the Brandenburg Gate
TypeSpeechGovernment photo
Photo — Source 09NARA, 12 Jun 1987
YEAR 1987  ·  DATE 12 June
SOURCE US National Archives · NARA ID 198585
PHOTO Reagan White House Photographic Office

“Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall!”

Spoken two and a half years before the Wall opened. Be careful with this one: it is famous, but historians disagree about how much it changed anything. Most argue the decisive pressure came from East Germans themselves in 1989. A useful source for showing that a memorable quotation is not the same as a cause.

commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Photograph_of_President_Reagan_giving_a_speech_at_the_Berlin_Wall,_Brandenburg_Gate,_Federal_Republic_of_Germany_-_NARA_-_198585.jpg
Source 10
Schabowski's press conference — “immediately, without delay”
TypeTranscriptPress conference
Photo — Source 10Brandenburg Gate, Nov 1989
YEAR 1989  ·  DATE 9 November, 18:53
SOURCE Chronik der Mauer (Federal Agency for Civic Education)
RECORD Verbatim transcript, from H.-H. Hertle

Journalist: “When does that come into force?” — Schabowski: “That takes effect, as far as I know … that is immediately, without delay.”

A word-for-word transcript, keeping every “er…” and the sound of him shuffling his papers. It shows the opening of the Wall was announced by accident by a spokesman reading a note he had not been briefed on. Set it against Erich Honecker on 18 January 1989, who said the Wall would “still exist in 50 and even in 100 years”.

chronik-der-mauer.de/material/184399/hans-hermann-hertle-9-november-1989-18-00-uhr-schabowskis-auftritt

Statistics

The evidence that is counted, not photographed

Numbers are a source type too — and like every other source, they were produced by someone, for a reason, using a definition they chose.

Source 11
East-to-West migration figures, 1949–1990
TypeStatisticsOfficial table
COVERS 1949–1990  ·  SOURCE German History in Documents and Images
331,390
1953 — PEAK YEAR
207,026
1961 — WALL BUILT
21,365
1962 — FIRST FULL YEAR
343,854
1989 — COLLAPSE

This is the single most powerful piece of evidence in the pack. The fall from 207,026 in 1961 to 21,365 in 1962 is the Wall's effect expressed as one number: measured against what it was built to do, it worked. The 1989 figure shows the system collapsing before the Wall was opened, not because of it.

germanhistorydocs.org/en/two-germanies-1961-1989/east-west-german-immigration-statistics-1961-1990
Source 12
Victims at the Berlin Wall — the research database
TypeDatabaseMuseum research
COVERS 1961–1989  ·  PUBLISHED 2017 / 2019
SOURCE Chronik der Mauer — Berlin Wall Foundation & Centre for Contemporary History (ZZF), Potsdam

At least 140 people were killed at the Wall: 101 who died trying to cross, 30 who were not fleeing plus one Soviet soldier, and 8 border guards. A further 251 travellers died at checkpoints. The team examined 576 cases and names every victim. Crucially, earlier counts ranged from 78 to over 200 — because each organisation counted different things. Use this to argue that even a hard number depends on the definition behind it.

chronik-der-mauer.de/en/victims/  ·  stiftung-berliner-mauer.de/en/topics/victims-berlin-wall

Section 5
Timeline & references

Timeline

1949–1961
About 2.7 million people leave East Germany for the West — roughly one in six of the population.
12–13 Aug 1961
“Barbed Wire Sunday.” Just after midnight, troops seal the border with wire and posts. Berlin wakes divided.
14 Aug 1961
The Brandenburg Gate is closed. It stays shut until 22 December 1989.
15 Aug 1961
Border guard Conrad Schumann jumps the wire into West Berlin — the escape becomes the most reproduced image of the Wall.
22 Aug 1961
Ida Siekmann dies jumping from her window on Bernauer Strasse — the first death at the Wall.
Oct 1961
Checkpoint Charlie stand-off. American and Soviet tanks face each other for sixteen hours.
26 Jun 1963
Kennedy tells a vast West Berlin crowd “Ich bin ein Berliner.”
12 Jun 1987
Reagan at the Brandenburg Gate: “Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall!”
18 Jan 1989
Honecker predicts the Wall “will still exist in 50 and even in 100 years.”
Autumn 1989
Mass Monday demonstrations across East Germany. 343,854 people leave for the West that year.
9 Nov 1989
At 18:53 Schabowski says the new travel rules apply “immediately, without delay.” Crowds force the crossings open that night.
3 Oct 1990
Germany is reunified, eleven months after the Wall opened.

How to use this pack on your chart

  1. Print pages 3–8. Cut along the dashed lines — each card is one complete piece of evidence.
  2. Glue the cards down the middle of the chart, oldest at the top, so the years run 1961 → 1989.
  3. Cut the definitions from page 2 into a column down one side.
  4. Put the source chart (page 10) at the bottom — that is the part showing source type and year.
  5. Add your own book photographs to page 9 before printing it, and write in the page numbers you quoted.
